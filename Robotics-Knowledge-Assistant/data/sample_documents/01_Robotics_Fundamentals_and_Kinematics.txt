1.1 Introduction to Robotics Fundamentals
A robot is a programmable, physical electromechanical system capable of sensing its environment, processing sensor feedback through embedded or host compute, and executing controlled mechanical motion via actuators. Core subsystems of an autonomous or articulated robot include:
1. Mechanical Structure (Links, Joints, Chassis, End-Effectors): Provides kinematic degrees of freedom (DOF) and structural stiffness. Revolute joints (R) permit rotational motion about an axis, whereas prismatic joints (P) permit linear translation.
2. Perception Subsystem: Proprioceptive sensors (quadrature encoders, potentiometers, IMUs) measure internal robot state; exteroceptive sensors (LiDAR, RGB-D cameras, ultrasonic rangefinders, force-torque wrists) measure external environment geometry.
3. Actuation & Power Transmission: DC brushed/brushless motors, stepper motors, servo drives, gearboxes (harmonic drives, planetary reducers), and pneumatic/hydraulic cylinders.
4. Compute & Control Architecture: Microcontrollers (Arduino, ESP32, STM32) for deterministic low-level real-time control loops, paired with onboard SBCs or industrial PCs running ROS2 for high-level planning and perception.

1.2 Degrees of Freedom and Workspace
A rigid body in 3D space has 6 degrees of freedom: three translational coordinates (x, y, z) and three rotational orientations (roll, pitch, yaw). A serial robotic manipulator typically requires at least 6 DOF to position and orient its end-effector arbitrarily within its dexterous workspace. Planar manipulators operate in 2D with 2 or 3 DOF. Redundant manipulators (7+ DOF, such as collaborative cobots) provide null-space motion to avoid joint limits and workspace obstacles while maintaining the end-effector pose.

1.3 Forward Kinematics and Homogeneous Transformations
Forward Kinematics (FK) computes the Cartesian position and orientation of the robot end-effector given a vector of joint variables q = [q1, q2, ..., qn]. Each consecutive link frame {i} relative to {i-1} is represented by a 4x4 homogeneous transformation matrix T containing a 3x3 rotation matrix R and a 3x1 translation vector p.

The Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) convention standardizes link-frame assignment using four parameters per joint:
- theta_i (joint angle): rotation about the Z_{i-1} axis from X_{i-1} to X_i (variable for revolute joints).
- d_i (link offset): translation along the Z_{i-1} axis from X_{i-1} to X_i (variable for prismatic joints).
- a_i (link length): translation along the X_i axis from Z_{i-1} to Z_i.
- alpha_i (link twist): rotation about the X_i axis from Z_{i-1} to Z_i.

By multiplying consecutive link matrices T_0^n = T_0^1 * T_1^2 * ... * T_{n-1}^n, the full end-effector pose relative to the robot base frame is obtained deterministically.

1.4 Inverse Kinematics, Jacobian, and Singularities
Inverse Kinematics (IK) solves the opposite problem: finding joint angles q that achieve a desired end-effector pose T_desired. Unlike forward kinematics, IK can have zero solutions (target outside workspace), multiple solutions (elbow-up vs. elbow-down configurations), or infinite solutions (kinematic redundancy). Analytical closed-form IK uses geometric trigonometry or algebraic decoupling (Pieper's solution for 6-DOF arms with spherical wrists), whereas numerical IK uses iterative damped least-squares (Levenberg-Marquardt) or Jacobian pseudo-inverse methods.

The Manipulator Jacobian J(q) maps joint velocities q_dot to end-effector linear and angular twists v = J(q) * q_dot. At kinematic singularities, det(J(q)) = 0 (or J loses rank), causing joint velocities to approach infinity for finite Cartesian motion. Designers avoid singularities using joint-space trajectory interpolation or damped pseudo-inverse regularization.
